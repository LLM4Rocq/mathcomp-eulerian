\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{About this document}

This document is a Stanley-style reading of the
\href{https://github.com/LLM4Rocq/mathcomp-eulerian}{\texttt{mathcomp-eulerian}}
formalization. Each definition and statement is given twice: once in
ordinary mathematical English (in the spirit of Stanley
\emph{Enumerative Combinatorics I}, Chapter~1) and once as the
verbatim Rocq text, with a clickable link to the line in the
repository on GitHub. The intention is that a mathematician can read
top to bottom like a textbook and at any point click through to
verify the formal statement.

\paragraph{Status of this prototype.} This first edition covers
Stanley \S1.4 (descents, Eulerian numbers, and the set-refined
count $\beps{n}{S}$), as Chapter~\ref{ch:descents}. It is preceded
by a short mathcomp primer (Chapter~\ref{ch:primer}) and followed
by a glossary appendix. Chapters covering \S1.3 (cycles, inversions,
Foata), the $q$-analogues, \S1.6.2 (longest alternating
subsequence), and \S1.6.3 (toggle action and the headline corollary)
are planned but not yet written.

\paragraph{First-time readers.} Read Chapter~\ref{ch:primer} once,
then Chapter~\ref{ch:descents} top to bottom. Use
Appendix~\ref{app:glossary} as a quick reference on second reading.
The primer and glossary together contain everything a Stanley reader
needs to know about mathcomp to verify the formal statements; you do
not need to install Rocq.

\paragraph{Conventions.} The single recurring shift is 0-indexing.
Stanley writes $w \in \Sn{n}$ for permutations of $\{1, \dots, n\}$
and $\descent(w) \subseteq \{1, \dots, n-1\}$ for the descent set;
we use $\Perm{\ord{n}}$ for permutations of
$\{0, \dots, n-1\}$ and $\Set{\ord{n-1}}$ for the descent set.
Concretely:
\[
  \text{our } \Perm{\ord{n+1}} \;=\; \text{Stanley's } \Sn{n+1}.
\]
A complete translation table is in
\href{https://github.com/LLM4Rocq/mathcomp-eulerian/blob/main/docs/READING_GUIDE.md}{\texttt{docs/READING\_GUIDE.md}};
a worked example through Stanley's $w = [3,1,4,2]$ is in the same file.

\paragraph{Reading the formal text.} Each Rocq block looks like
this:

\rocqsource{descent.v}{25}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Definition descent_set s : {set 'I_n} := [set i | is_descent s i].
\end{lstlisting}
\decoded{$\descent(s) = \{\,i \in \{0, \dots, n-1\} : s(i) > s(i+1)\,\}$.}

\noindent The bracketed link at the top is clickable and opens the
exact line on GitHub. The Rocq body is verbatim; identifiers and
keywords are highlighted but no mathematical substitution has been
performed. The shaded \emph{Decoded} line below the block restates
the formal text in plain mathematics, mechanically substituting
mathcomp casts for their meaning. Side by side, the two should say
the same thing; if they do not, that is exactly the kind of
discrepancy worth flagging.

\noindent Some definitions also carry a \textsf{Design choice}
sidebar that explains why a particular formalization was chosen
when a more natural-looking alternative exists. These boxes are
for first reading and can be skipped on a re-read.

\paragraph{What is \emph{not} included here.}
Full proofs are not included; only the formal \emph{statements} are
shown alongside the informal mathematics, with a one-paragraph proof
sketch for each non-trivial result. The full proof is always one
click away in the linked source. The cd-index development
(\texttt{mmtree.v}, \texttt{psi\_*.v}) is project-internal scaffolding
not in Stanley and is not covered.

\paragraph{Verifying axiom-freeness.} Every theorem in this document
is kernel-validated by Rocq with no axioms or admits. To check this
yourself for, e.g., the headline Corollary~\ref{cor:beta_alt_max}:

\begin{verbatim}

      Print Assumptions beta_alt_max.' | rocq top -R . mathcomp_eulerian
\end{verbatim}

\noindent prints \texttt{Closed under the global context}, meaning no
axiom is used anywhere in its transitive dependencies.

\chapter{A combinatorialist's mathcomp primer}
\label{ch:primer}
% =====================================================================

This chapter is a focused reference for the few mathcomp primitives
that appear in Chapter~\ref{ch:descents}. It is not a tutorial; it
gives just enough vocabulary to read the formal blocks and recognise
that they say what the informal text claims. Skim it once on first
read; come back to it as a glossary when re-reading.

% =====================================================================
\section{Ordinals: the type \texttt{'I\_n}}
% =====================================================================

The type \texttt{'I\_n} (read ``I-sub-n'') is mathcomp's
\emph{finite type} of natural numbers strictly less than $n$. An
inhabitant is a pair: a natural number \texttt{i : nat} and a
\emph{proof} \texttt{i\,<\,n}. The two together are written
\texttt{Ordinal i (proof : i\,<\,n)} but a reader normally never
constructs ordinals by hand --- the type appears as the index of a
sum or a permutation domain, and inhabitants come from the typing
context.

\begin{description}
  \item[\texttt{ord0 : 'I\_n.+1}] the value $0$, with its proof
    $0 < n+1$. Always available when the bound is at least $1$.
  \item[\texttt{ord\_max : 'I\_n.+1}] the value $n$, with its proof.
    The largest element of \texttt{'I\_n.+1}.
  \item[\texttt{val i : nat}] strips an ordinal back to its
    underlying natural number, forgetting the proof.
\end{description}

\noindent The \emph{cardinality} of \texttt{'I\_n} is exactly $n$
(lemma \texttt{card\_ord} in \texttt{fintype.v}). So
\texttt{\#|'I\_4|\,=\,4}.

% =====================================================================
\section{Casts: \texttt{widen\_ord}, \texttt{lift}, \texttt{unlift}}
\label{sec:casts}
% =====================================================================

There are exactly two natural maps $\ord{n} \to \ord{n+1}$:

\begin{description}
  \item[\texttt{widen\_ord (h : n \,\textbackslash leq\, n+1) i}]
    sends \texttt{i : 'I\_n} to the same numerical value, viewed
    inside the bigger type. Diagrammatically:
    \begin{center}
      $i : \ord{n} \quad\xrightarrow{\;\texttt{widen\_ord}\;}\quad i : \ord{n+1}.$
    \end{center}
    The $h$ argument is a proof that $n \leq n+1$, supplied by
    \texttt{leqnSn n}. Mathcomp users write
    \texttt{widen\_ord (leqnSn n) i} reflexively.

  \item[\texttt{lift (p : 'I\_n.+1) i}]
    sends \texttt{i : 'I\_n} to a value in \texttt{'I\_n.+1}
    \emph{skipping} the value $p$. With $p = \texttt{ord0}$, this
    shifts every value up by one:
    \begin{center}
      $i : \ord{n} \quad\xrightarrow{\;\texttt{lift ord0}\;}\quad i+1 : \ord{n+1}.$
    \end{center}
    The general \texttt{lift p i} skips $p$, which is exactly what
    one needs when removing one position from a permutation.
\end{description}

\noindent The two casts together name two specific elements of
\texttt{'I\_n.+1} from the same source $i : \ord{n}$: the value $i$
itself, and the value $i+1$. This is the idiom that lets us write
``the comparison between consecutive positions $i$ and $i+1$''
without ever leaving the well-typed world of finite ordinals.

The partial inverse of \texttt{lift p} is \texttt{unlift p}, of
type \texttt{'I\_n.+1\,\textbackslash to\,option 'I\_n}. Concretely:
\texttt{unlift p i = None} if \texttt{i = p}, and
\texttt{unlift p i = Some j} otherwise, with $j$ the unique witness
of \texttt{i = lift p j}. This is the canonical Rocq idiom for the
case-split ``is the input the special index $p$, or one of the
others?'' --- it appears in every inductive bijection on ordinals.

% =====================================================================
\section{Finite sets: \texttt{\{set T\}}}
% =====================================================================

For \texttt{T} a finite type (such as \texttt{'I\_n}),
\texttt{\{set T\}} is the type of \emph{finite subsets of T}, with
\emph{extensional} equality: two sets are equal iff they have the
same elements.

\begin{description}
  \item[\texttt{[set i : T\ |\ P i]}] set comprehension --- the set
    of \texttt{i : T} satisfying the boolean predicate $P$. The
    vertical bar reads as ``such that'', as in mathematical notation.
  \item[\texttt{\#|S|}] the cardinality of \texttt{S} as a natural
    number.
  \item[\texttt{i \textbackslash in S}] membership test, returning
    a \texttt{bool}.
  \item[\texttt{\textasciitilde:\,S}] complement, \texttt{S\,:|:\,T}
    union, \texttt{S\,:\&:\,T} intersection, \texttt{S\,:\textbackslash:\,T}
    difference.
\end{description}

\noindent \texttt{\{set T\}} is itself a finite type, which means
big operators (sums, products) can be indexed by sets. This matters
for the Eulerian recurrence and for the partition-by-descent-set
identity \texttt{beta\_eulerian}.

% =====================================================================
\section{Permutations: \texttt{\{perm T\}}}
% =====================================================================

\texttt{\{perm T\}} is the type of \emph{bijections} \texttt{T \textbackslash to T}
for \texttt{T} a finite type. An inhabitant is a function
together with a proof of bijectivity, packaged so that:

\begin{description}
  \item[\texttt{s i}] applies the permutation \texttt{s} to
    \texttt{i : T} as a function. (No special syntax is needed:
    \texttt{\{perm T\}} \emph{coerces} to a function.)
  \item[\texttt{1\%g}] is the identity permutation. The \texttt{\%g}
    selects mathcomp's group scope.
  \item[\texttt{(s * t)\%g i}] is composition. Mathcomp's convention
    is that \texttt{(s * t) i = t (s i)} (right-to-left for the
    \emph{action}; the lemma is \texttt{permM}).
  \item[\texttt{perm (h : injective f)}] constructs a permutation
    from an injective function \texttt{f : T \textbackslash to T} (on a finite
    type, injective implies bijective).
\end{description}

\noindent The cardinality of \texttt{\{perm 'I\_n\}} is $n!$
(Stanley's $|\Sn{n}|$); this is the lemma \texttt{card\_perm} or
\texttt{card\_Sn} in mathcomp.

% =====================================================================
\section{Big operators: \texttt{\textbackslash sum}, \texttt{\textbackslash prod}}
% =====================================================================

Mathcomp's \texttt{bigop.v} provides indexed sums and products with
a uniform syntax:

\begin{description}
  \item[\texttt{\textbackslash sum\_(i\ :\ T) f i}] sums \texttt{f i}
    over every \texttt{i : T} (\texttt{T} a finite type).
  \item[\texttt{\textbackslash sum\_(i\ <\ n) f i}] same, with
    \texttt{i : 'I\_n}.
  \item[\texttt{\textbackslash sum\_(i\ \textbackslash in\ S) f i}]
    sums over an explicit set \texttt{S}.
  \item[\texttt{\textbackslash sum\_(i\ |\ P i) f i}] sums over
    indices satisfying the boolean filter \texttt{P}.
\end{description}

\noindent The vertical bar in \texttt{\textbackslash sum\_(i\ |\ P i)}
is a \emph{filter}, not the disjunction symbol. Replace
\texttt{\textbackslash sum} by \texttt{\textbackslash prod} for
products. Several recurring \emph{moves} appear in the proofs:

\begin{description}
  \item[\texttt{partition\_big f xpredT}] rewrite
    \texttt{\textbackslash sum\_x g x} as
    \texttt{\textbackslash sum\_y \textbackslash sum\_(x\ |\ f x = y) g x}
    --- partition by the value of $f$.
  \item[\texttt{bigID p}] split a sum into the part where the
    predicate $p$ holds and the part where it does not.
  \item[\texttt{reindex \ldots}] change of summation variable along
    a bijection.
\end{description}

\noindent These names appear in the proofs we will not reprint in
this document, but they are worth recognising if you click through
to a \texttt{.v} file.

% =====================================================================
\section{Sections and section variables}
% =====================================================================

Every formalization file in this project opens with

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Section X.
Variable n : nat.
Definition foo := ...    (* implicitly takes n *)
Lemma bar : ...          (* implicitly takes n *)
End X.
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent Inside the section, definitions and lemmas may use
\texttt{n} as if it were globally fixed. At \texttt{End X}, every
definition and lemma is automatically generalised to take \texttt{n}
as an explicit argument. This is why \texttt{descent.v} reads
\texttt{Variable n : nat} once at the top and then
\texttt{Definition is\_descent s i := ...} without an explicit
\texttt{n}, but outside the file \texttt{is\_descent} is invoked as
\texttt{is\_descent n s i}. (For the mathematician: think of
\texttt{Variable} as ``Let \texttt{n} be a natural number'' at the
start of a chapter.)

% =====================================================================
\section{The takeaway}
% =====================================================================

Three points are enough to read Chapter~\ref{ch:descents}:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \texttt{\{perm 'I\_n.+1\}} is mathcomp's $\Sn{n+1}$. Apply
    a permutation to a position by writing \texttt{s i}; the descent
    set is a \texttt{\{set 'I\_n\}} (positions, not values).
  \item The two casts \texttt{widen\_ord (leqnSn n) i} and
    \texttt{lift ord0 i} pick out positions $i$ and $i+1$ inside
    \texttt{'I\_n.+1} from a single $i : \ord{n}$. The reader does
    not need to verify cast arithmetic by hand --- the kernel
    rejects the file unless every cast is the unique one of its
    type.
  \item Big operators \texttt{\textbackslash sum\_(\ldots)} are
    just sums; the syntax variants control the index range. The
    vertical bar is a filter.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent With these in hand, the formal blocks in
Chapter~\ref{ch:descents} read directly. Each formal block is
followed by a one-line \emph{Decoded} reading that mechanically
substitutes the mathcomp casts for their mathematical meaning, so
the reader has both forms side by side.

\chapter{Descents and Eulerian numbers}
\label{ch:descents}
% =====================================================================

This chapter formalizes Stanley \S1.4 (Stanley
\emph{Enumerative Combinatorics I}, 2nd ed., pp.~30--38). The central
objects are the \emph{descent set} of a permutation, the
\emph{Eulerian numbers} $\eulerianA(n, k)$ that count permutations by
descent count, and the set-refined count $\beps{n}{S}$ that counts
permutations by their full descent set.

% =====================================================================
\section{The descent set}
\label{sec:descent_set}
% =====================================================================

\begin{definition}[Descent]
  \label{def:is_descent}
  Let $w = (w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n) \in \Sn{n}$. A position
  $i \in \{1, \dots, n-1\}$ is a \emph{descent} of $w$ if
  $w_i > w_{i+1}$.
\end{definition}

\rocqsource{descent.v}{22}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Definition is_descent s i : bool :=
  s (widen_ord (leqnSn n) i) > s (lift ord0 i).
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{$s(i) > s(i+1)$ for $i \in \{0, \dots, n-1\}$ and
$s : \{0, \dots, n\} \to \{0, \dots, n\}$ a bijection.}

\begin{sidebar}{why \texttt{widen\_ord} and \texttt{lift}, not \texttt{s i > s i.+1}?}
The naive form \texttt{s i > s i.+1} does not type-check. The
permutation \texttt{s : \{perm 'I\_n.+1\}} expects an argument of
type \texttt{'I\_n.+1}, but a descent slot $i$ lives in
\texttt{'I\_n} (there are $n$ adjacencies for $n+1$ positions).
The two casts \texttt{widen\_ord (leqnSn n) i} and
\texttt{lift ord0 i} are the canonical maps that ship $i$ into the
larger type \texttt{'I\_n.+1} as the values $i$ and $i+1$
respectively (Primer~\ref{sec:casts}). There is no way to ``just
cast'' --- the two casts encode \emph{which} element of
\texttt{'I\_n.+1} we mean. The verbosity is what makes the
formalization correct by typing.
\end{sidebar}

\noindent The result type \texttt{bool} (rather than \texttt{Prop})
makes \texttt{is\_descent} computable: \texttt{Compute is\_descent s i}
returns \texttt{true} or \texttt{false} for any concrete \texttt{s}
and \texttt{i}. Mathcomp's small-scale reflection bridges
\texttt{is\_descent\,s\,i\,=\,true} with the corresponding
proposition on demand.

\begin{definition}[Descent set]
  \label{def:descent_set}
  The \emph{descent set} of $w \in \Sn{n}$ is
  \[
    \descent(w) \;=\; \{\, i \in \{1, \dots, n-1\} : w_i > w_{i+1} \,\}.
  \]
\end{definition}

\rocqsource{descent.v}{25}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Definition descent_set s : {set 'I_n} := [set i | is_descent s i].
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{$\descent(s) = \{\,i \in \{0, \dots, n-1\} : s(i) > s(i+1)\,\}$.}

\begin{sidebar}{why \texttt{\{set 'I\_n\}}, not \texttt{pred 'I\_n} or \texttt{seq 'I\_n}?}
Three options exist for representing a finite collection of
positions: a boolean predicate (\texttt{pred T}), a list
(\texttt{seq T}), or a set (\texttt{\{set T\}}).
\texttt{\{set\,'I\_n\}} is the right choice here for two reasons.
First, it has \emph{extensional equality}: two sets are equal iff
they have the same elements, so \texttt{descent\_set s = D} is the
condition we want, with no axiom of function extensionality
required. Second, \texttt{\{set 'I\_n\}} is itself a finite type,
which means we can sum over it with mathcomp's big operators ---
the lemma \texttt{beta\_eulerian} below uses
\texttt{\textbackslash sum\_(D : \{set 'I\_n\}\,|\,\#|D|\,==\,k)}
exactly because \texttt{\{set\}} supports this.
\texttt{pred 'I\_n} fails the first test (extensional equality of
predicates needs \texttt{funext}); \texttt{seq 'I\_n} carries
spurious order and multiplicity.
\end{sidebar}

\noindent
$\descent(w)$ is a subset of $\{1, \dots, n-1\}$ in Stanley; in our
types it is a subset of $\{0, \dots, n-1\} = \ord{n}$. The
correspondence is $\text{Stanley's } i \leftrightarrow \text{our } (i-1)$.

\begin{example}[{Stanley's $w = [3,1,4,2]$, both ways}]
  \label{ex:s3142}
  Take $w = [3, 1, 4, 2] \in \Sn{4}$. Then
  $\descent(w) = \{1, 3\}$ since $w_1 = 3 > 1 = w_2$ and
  $w_3 = 4 > 2 = w_4$. In our types, the corresponding
  $s : \Perm{\ord{4}}$ has $s\,0 = 2$, $s\,1 = 0$, $s\,2 = 3$,
  $s\,3 = 1$ (values shifted to $\{0,1,2,3\}$). Then:
  \begin{computestrip}
Compute is_descent s 0.    (* = true, since s 0 = 2 > 0 = s 1 *)
Compute is_descent s 1.    (* = false, since s 1 = 0 < 3 = s 2 *)
Compute is_descent s 2.    (* = true, since s 2 = 3 > 1 = s 3 *)
Compute descent_set s.     (* = [set 0; 2] : {set 'I_3} *)
Compute des s.             (* = 2 *)
  \end{computestrip}
  \noindent After the index shift our $\{0, 2\}$ corresponds to
  Stanley's $\{1, 3\}$, and $\des(s) = 2 = \des(w)$. The
  computation is what the kernel does on the elaborated term ---
  the formal definition matches the informal one not by editorial
  promise but by the test running here on the page.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[Descent number, ascent number]
  \label{def:des_asc}
  $\des(w) = |\descent(w)|$ and $\asc(w) = (n-1) - \des(w)$.
\end{definition}

\rocqsource{descent.v}{27}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Definition des s : nat := #|descent_set s|.
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{$\des(s) = |\descent(s)|$, the number of descent positions.}

\rocqsource{descent.v}{29}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Definition asc s : nat := n - des s.
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{$\asc(s) = n - \des(s)$, the number of ascent positions
(here $n$ is the number of descent slots, i.e.\ Stanley's $n-1$).}

\begin{sidebar}{why is the parameter \texttt{n} ``perm size minus one''?}
A permutation of $n+1$ elements has $n$ adjacencies, so the
descent set lives in $\{0, \dots, n-1\}$ regardless of how we
parameterise. Two choices: name the parameter after the perm size
(forcing every descent-set type to be \texttt{\{set 'I\_n.-1\}}
with a \texttt{.-1}), or name it after the descent slot count
(forcing the perm type to be \texttt{\{perm 'I\_n.+1\}} with a
\texttt{.+1}). We picked the latter because it removes a
\texttt{.-1} from every theorem about descent sets and from every
\texttt{eulerian n k} statement. The cost is a single conversion:
\textbf{our parameter \texttt{n} is Stanley's $n-1$}, and our
\texttt{eulerian\,n\,k} is Stanley's $\eulerianA(n+1, k)$. This
is a project convention, not a mathcomp requirement; mathcomp
itself uses both styles in different files. We flag the off-by-one
explicitly at every theorem statement that crosses it.
\end{sidebar}

\begin{lemma}[Descent count is bounded]
  \label{lem:des_le}
  $\des(w) \leq n - 1$ for every $w \in \Sn{n}$.
\end{lemma}

\rocqsource{descent.v}{38}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Lemma des_le s : des s <= n.
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{$\des(s) \leq n$ for every $s : \Perm{\ord{n+1}}$
(equivalently $\des(w) \leq |w| - 1$).}

\noindent
\emph{Proof sketch.} $\descent(w) \subseteq \{0, \dots, n-1\}$ which
has $n$ elements; the cardinality is at most $n$. \qed

\begin{lemma}[Identity has no descents]
  \label{lem:des_id}
  $\des(\mathrm{id}) = 0$.
\end{lemma}

\rocqsource{descent.v}{44}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Lemma des_id : des (1 : {perm 'I_n.+1}) = 0.
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{$\des(\mathrm{id}) = 0$, where \texttt{1\%g} is the group
identity, i.e.\ the identity permutation.}

\noindent
\emph{Proof sketch.} For the identity, $s\,i = i$ at every position,
so $s(i) = i < i+1 = s(i+1)$ --- no descent slot. \qed

% =====================================================================
\section{Reversal symmetry}
\label{sec:reversal}
% =====================================================================

A useful symmetry: reversing a permutation flips ascents and
descents at corresponding positions.

\begin{definition}[Reverse permutation]
  \label{def:rev_perm}
  Let $w \in \Sn{n}$ and define $w^{\mathrm{rev}}$ by
  $w^{\mathrm{rev}}_i = w_{n+1-i}$.
\end{definition}

\rocqsource{descent.v}{80}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Definition rev_perm_ord : {perm 'I_n.+1} := perm (@rev_ord_inj n.+1).
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{The position-reversal involution $i \mapsto n - i$,
packaged as a permutation. \texttt{perm (h : injective f)} is the
constructor that turns an injective function into a
\texttt{\{perm T\}}.}

\rocqsource{descent.v}{85}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Definition rev_perm s : {perm 'I_n.+1} := rev_perm_ord * s.
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{$w^{\mathrm{rev}}(i) = s(n - i)$, the reversal of $s$
viewed at the level of positions.}

\begin{sidebar}{why \texttt{rev\_perm\_ord * s}, not \texttt{s * rev\_perm\_ord}?}
Mathcomp's group convention is \texttt{(s * t) i = t (s i)} (see
the lemma \texttt{permM}). With \texttt{rev\_perm := rev\_perm\_ord * s},
applying gives \texttt{(rev\_perm\_ord * s) i = s (rev\_perm\_ord i)
= s (n - i)}. This reverses \emph{positions} (Stanley's
$w^{\mathrm{rev}}_i = w_{n+1-i}$). Post-composing instead would
reverse \emph{values}, a different operation. Picking the wrong
side here would silently change the lemma's content; the choice
matches Stanley by direct check on a small example.
\end{sidebar}

\begin{lemma}[Descents of the reverse]
  \label{lem:is_descent_rev}
  Position $i$ is a descent of $w^{\mathrm{rev}}$ iff position $n - i$
  is \emph{not} a descent of $w$:
  \[
    i \in \descent(w^{\mathrm{rev}})
    \iff
    (n - i) \notin \descent(w).
  \]
\end{lemma}

\rocqsource{descent.v}{90}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Lemma is_descent_rev s (i : 'I_n) :
  is_descent (rev_perm s) i = ~~ is_descent s (rev_ord i).
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{Position $i$ is a descent of $w^{\mathrm{rev}}$ iff
position $n - i$ is \emph{not} a descent of $w$. The
\texttt{\textasciitilde\textasciitilde} is boolean negation.}

\noindent
\emph{Proof sketch.} Direct calculation: $\mathtt{(rev\_perm\,s)\,j} =
s(n-j)$, and the comparison $s(n-i) > s(n-i-1)$ is the negation of
$s(n-i-1) > s(n-i)$. \qed

\section{Eulerian numbers}
\label{sec:eulerian}
% =====================================================================

\begin{definition}[Eulerian number]
  \label{def:eulerian}
  The \emph{Eulerian number} $\eulerianA(n, k)$ is the number of
  permutations of $\{1, \dots, n\}$ with exactly $k$ descents:
  \[
    \eulerianA(n, k) \;=\; \#\{\, w \in \Sn{n} : \des(w) = k \,\}.
  \]
\end{definition}

\rocqsource{eulerian.v}{14}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Definition eulerian (n k : nat) : nat :=
  #|[set s : {perm 'I_n.+1} | des s == k]|.
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{$\texttt{eulerian}\,n\,k = |\{s \in \Sn{n+1} : \des(s) = k\}|$.
The \texttt{==} is boolean equality on \texttt{nat}, and
\texttt{\#|\ldots|} is set cardinality.}

\noindent
\textbf{Off-by-one alert.} The Rocq parameter $n$ is ``permutation
size minus one'' (see the design-choice sidebar above). Concretely,
\texttt{eulerian n k} counts perms of $\Perm{\ord{n+1}}$, which are
Stanley's $\Sn{n+1}$. Hence
\[
  \texttt{eulerian } n\ k \;=\; \eulerianA(n+1, k).
\]
For instance, \texttt{eulerian 3 1} corresponds to Stanley's
$\eulerianA(4, 1) = 11$ (Stanley table p.~36).

\begin{computestrip}
Compute eulerian 0 0.    (* = 1     -- Stanley A(1,0) = 1 *)
Compute eulerian 1 0.    (* = 1     -- Stanley A(2,0) = 1 *)
Compute eulerian 1 1.    (* = 1     -- Stanley A(2,1) = 1 *)
Compute eulerian 2 0.    (* = 1     -- Stanley A(3,0) = 1 *)
Compute eulerian 2 1.    (* = 4     -- Stanley A(3,1) = 4 *)
Compute eulerian 2 2.    (* = 1     -- Stanley A(3,2) = 1 *)
\end{computestrip}

\noindent The numbers match Stanley's table on p.~36. Computing
\texttt{eulerian} on \texttt{\{perm 'I\_n.+1\}} requires enumerating
the set; for $n \geq 4$ the brute-force enumeration is slow, but
for the small cases above it returns immediately and the values
agree.

\begin{lemma}[Row sum]
  \label{lem:eulerian_row_sum}
  $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \eulerianA(n, k) = n!$
\end{lemma}

\rocqsource{eulerian.v}{31}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Lemma eulerian_row_sum_fact n :
  \sum_(k < n.+1) eulerian n k = n.+1`!.
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{$\sum_{k=0}^{n} \eulerianA(n+1, k) = (n+1)!$. The
\texttt{`!} is mathcomp's factorial notation; \texttt{\textbackslash sum\_(k\,<\,n.+1)}
sums over $k \in \{0, \dots, n\}$.}

\noindent
\emph{Proof sketch.} The summands partition $\Sn{n+1}$ by descent
count; the total is $|\Sn{n+1}| = (n+1)!$. \qed

\begin{lemma}[Out-of-range vanishing]
  \label{lem:eulerian_out_of_range}
  $\eulerianA(n, k) = 0$ for $k \geq n$.
\end{lemma}

\rocqsource{eulerian.v}{34}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Lemma eulerian_out_of_range n k :
  n < k -> eulerian n k = 0.
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{If $k > n$ then $\eulerianA(n+1, k) = 0$, since perms of
length $n+1$ have at most $n$ descents.}

\noindent
\emph{Proof sketch.} A permutation has at most $n$ descent positions
(Lemma~\ref{lem:des_le}); none can have $k > n$ descents. \qed

\begin{lemma}[The unique perm with no descents is the identity]
  \label{lem:des0_id}
  If $\des(w) = 0$ then $w = \mathrm{id}$.
\end{lemma}

\rocqsource{eulerian.v}{41}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Lemma des0_id n (s : {perm 'I_n.+1}) : des s = 0 -> s = 1%g.
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{If $\des(s) = 0$ then $s$ is the identity permutation.}

\noindent
\emph{Proof sketch.} $\des(s) = 0$ means $s(i) < s(i+1)$ for every
adjacent pair $i$, so $s$ is strictly increasing. The only strictly
increasing function $\ord{n+1} \to \ord{n+1}$ is the identity. \qed

\section{The insert-max bijection}
\label{sec:insert_max}
% =====================================================================

A central tool for the Eulerian recurrence is the bijection that
inserts the maximum value $n+1$ at a chosen position into a
permutation of $\{1, \dots, n\}$.

\begin{definition}[Insert-max]
  \label{def:insert_max}
  For $t \in \Sn{n}$ and $p \in \{1, \dots, n+1\}$, let
  $\mathtt{insert\_max}(t, p) \in \Sn{n+1}$ be the permutation
  obtained by inserting the value $n+1$ at position $p$ in the
  one-line form of $t$.
\end{definition}

\rocqsourcerange{eulerian.v}{134}{157}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Definition insert_max_fun (i : 'I_n.+2) : 'I_n.+2 :=
  match unlift p i with
  | Some j => widen_ord (leqnSn _) (t j)
  | None => ord_max
  end.

Definition insert_max_perm : {perm 'I_n.+2} := perm insert_max_fun_inj.
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{For $i \in \{0, \dots, n+1\}$: if $i = p$, output $n+1$
(the new max); otherwise, write $i = $ \texttt{lift p j} for the
unique $j \in \{0, \dots, n\}$ that skips $p$, and output $t(j)$
cast into the larger type.}

\begin{sidebar}{why \texttt{match unlift p i with}?}
The fundamental case-split for inserting at position $p$ is ``is
this position $p$ or not?''. Mathcomp encodes this as the
\texttt{option}-valued partial inverse \texttt{unlift p}: it
returns \texttt{None} exactly when $i = p$, and
\texttt{Some j} otherwise --- where $j : \ord{n+1}$ is the unique
witness of $i = $ \texttt{lift p j}. Carrying $j$ in the
\texttt{Some} branch (rather than recomputing it from $i$ and $p$)
avoids any arithmetic juggling: we get the correct
\texttt{'I\_n.+1} value with its bound proof for free, and can
apply $t$ directly. This is the universal idiom for constructing
permutations on \texttt{'I\_n.+2} from a permutation on
\texttt{'I\_n.+1} plus a special index.
\end{sidebar}

\begin{definition}[Extract-max]
  \label{def:extract_max}
  The inverse: given $\sigma \in \Sn{n+1}$ with $\sigma(p) = n+1$
  for some position $p$, remove that value and standardise to obtain
  $t \in \Sn{n}$.
\end{definition}

\rocqsourcerange{eulerian.v}{185}{203}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Definition extract_max_fun (j : 'I_n.+1) : 'I_n.+1 :=
  odflt j (unlift ord_max (s (lift p j))).

Definition extract_max_perm : {perm 'I_n.+1} := perm extract_max_fun_inj.
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{For $j \in \{0, \dots, n\}$: look at $s$ at position $j$
of the original (skipping $p$); the result is some value in
$\{0, \dots, n+1\}$ that is not $n+1$ (because $\sigma(p) = n+1$
and $\sigma$ is a bijection); strip the high bit to land in
$\{0, \dots, n\}$. The \texttt{odflt j (...)} provides a default
that is provably unreachable.}

\begin{theorem}[Insert-max is a bijection]
  \label{thm:insert_max_bij}
  The map $(t, p) \mapsto \mathtt{insert\_max}(t, p)$ is a bijection
  $\Sn{n} \times \{1, \dots, n+1\} \to \Sn{n+1}$.
\end{theorem}

\rocqsource{eulerian.v}{437}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Lemma insert_max_perm_bij n :
  forall sigma : {perm 'I_n.+2},
    exists! tp : {perm 'I_n.+1} * 'I_n.+2,
      sigma = insert_max_perm tp.1 tp.2.
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{Every $\sigma \in \Sn{n+2}$ arises uniquely as
$\mathtt{insert\_max}(t, p)$ for some $(t, p) \in \Sn{n+1} \times \{0, \dots, n+1\}$.}

\noindent
\emph{Proof sketch.} Given $\sigma$, the unique $p$ is
$\sigma^{-1}(n+1)$ and $t = \mathtt{extract\_max}(\sigma, p)$. The
two roundtrips are direct calculations. \qed

\section{Eulerian recurrence}
\label{sec:eulerian_rec}
% =====================================================================

\begin{theorem}[Eulerian recurrence]
  \label{thm:eulerian_rec}
  For $n \geq 1$ and $0 \leq k \leq n$,
  \[
    \eulerianA(n+1, k)
    \;=\;
    (k+1)\,\eulerianA(n, k) \;+\; (n - k + 1)\,\eulerianA(n, k-1).
  \]
\end{theorem}

\rocqsource{eulerian.v}{458}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Theorem eulerian_rec n k :
  eulerian n.+1 k =
    (k.+1) * eulerian n k + (n.+1 - k) * eulerian n k.-1.
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{$\eulerianA(n+2, k) = (k+1)\,\eulerianA(n+1, k) + (n+1 - k)\,\eulerianA(n+1, k-1)$.
The \texttt{n.+1\,-\,k} on the second term reads as ``$n+1-k$''
under our parameterisation; compare to Stanley's $(n+1-k)$ in
$A(n+2, k) = (k+1) A(n+1, k) + (n+1 - k)\,A(n+1, k-1)$.}

\noindent
\emph{Proof sketch.} Using the insert-max bijection, classify each
$\sigma \in \Sn{n+1}$ by the position $p$ of $n+1$. There are
$k+1$ ascent positions and $n - k$ descent positions where inserting
$n+1$ preserves the descent count; inserting at the other $n - k + 1$
slots increases it by one. Summing recovers the recurrence. \qed

\section{The set-refined count $\beta$}
\label{sec:beta}
% =====================================================================

The descent count is a coarse invariant. The full descent
\emph{set} carries more information; the count of permutations with
a fixed descent set is a finer object.

\begin{definition}[$\beta$ count]
  \label{def:beta}
  For a subset $S \subseteq \{1, \dots, n-1\}$, the count
  $\beps{n}{S}$ is the number of permutations of $\{1, \dots, n\}$
  whose descent set is exactly $S$:
  \[
    \beps{n}{S} \;=\; \#\{\, w \in \Sn{n} : \descent(w) = S \,\}.
  \]
\end{definition}

\rocqsource{beta.v}{19}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Definition beta (n : nat) (D : {set 'I_n}) : nat :=
  #|[set sigma : {perm 'I_n.+1} | descent_set sigma == D]|.
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{$\texttt{beta}\,n\,D = |\{\sigma \in \Sn{n+1} : \descent(\sigma) = D\}|$,
the number of perms of length $n+1$ with descent set exactly $D$.}

\noindent
\textbf{Off-by-one alert.} As with \texttt{eulerian}, the Rocq
parameter $n$ is ``perm size minus one.'' So
\texttt{beta n D} for $D : \Set{\ord{n}}$ equals Stanley's
$\beps{n+1}{D}$, with $D$ interpreted as a subset of $\{0, \dots, n-1\}$
that corresponds to Stanley's 1-indexed $S \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}$.

\begin{lemma}[$\beta$ partitions Eulerian]
  \label{lem:beta_eulerian}
  Summing $\beps{n}{S}$ over all $S$ of cardinality $k$ recovers the
  Eulerian number:
  \[
    \sum_{|S| = k} \beps{n}{S} \;=\; \eulerianA(n, k).
  \]
\end{lemma}

\rocqsource{beta.v}{124}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Lemma beta_eulerian n k :
  \sum_(D : {set 'I_n} | #|D| == k) beta D = eulerian n k.
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{$\sum_{D \subseteq \{0, \dots, n-1\},\ |D| = k} \beps{n+1}{D} = \eulerianA(n+1, k)$.
The big-op syntax \texttt{\textbackslash sum\_(D\,:\,\{set 'I\_n\}\,|\,\#|D|\,==\,k)}
sums over all sets $D$ of cardinality exactly $k$.}

\noindent
\emph{Proof sketch.} The sets $\{\sigma : \descent(\sigma) = D\}$ for
$D$ ranging over subsets of cardinality $k$ partition the set of
permutations with $k$ descents. The cardinalities sum. \qed

\begin{lemma}[$\beta$ is reversal-invariant]
  \label{lem:beta_rev}
  For $S \subseteq \{1, \dots, n-1\}$, let $S^{\mathrm{rev}} =
  \{n - i : i \in S\}$. Then
  $\beps{n}{S^{\mathrm{rev}}} = \beps{n}{S^{\mathrm{c}}}$,
  where $S^{\mathrm{c}}$ is the complement in $\{1, \dots, n-1\}$.
\end{lemma}

\rocqsource{beta.v}{140}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Rocq]
Lemma beta_rev n (D : {set 'I_n}) :
  beta [set rev_ord i | i in D] = beta (~: D).
\end{lstlisting}

\decoded{$\beps{n+1}{\{n-1-i : i \in D\}} = \beps{n+1}{\overline{D}}$.
The set comprehension \texttt{[set rev\_ord i | i in D]} is the
image of $D$ under $i \mapsto n-1-i$; \texttt{\textasciitilde:\,D}
is the complement of $D$ in \texttt{'I\_n}.}

\noindent
\emph{Proof sketch.} Reversal $w \mapsto w^{\mathrm{rev}}$ is a
bijection on $\Sn{n}$; by Lemma~\ref{lem:is_descent_rev} it maps
descent set $D$ to $\{n - i : i \notin D\}$, which is the
complement of $\{n - i : i \in D\}$. Counting on both sides
matches. \qed

\chapter{Glossary of mathcomp primitives}
\label{app:glossary}
% =====================================================================

A quick-reference companion to Chapter~\ref{ch:primer}. Entries are
ordered roughly by first appearance in the chapter; each gives the
mathcomp source file, a one-line gloss, and an example.

\begin{description}
  \item[\texttt{'I\_n}] (\texttt{fintype.v}) The finite type of
    natural numbers strictly less than $n$. Inhabitant: a pair
    \texttt{(i, proof of i\,<\,n)}. Cardinality $n$.

  \item[\texttt{ord0\,:\,'I\_n.+1}] (\texttt{fintype.v}) The value
    $0$, with its proof of $0 < n+1$.

  \item[\texttt{ord\_max\,:\,'I\_n.+1}] (\texttt{fintype.v}) The
    value $n$, the largest element.

  \item[\texttt{val\,i\,:\,nat}] (\texttt{eqtype.v}) Strips an
    ordinal back to its underlying natural number.

  \item[\texttt{widen\_ord (h\,:\,m\,\textbackslash leq\,n) i}]
    (\texttt{fintype.v}) Casts \texttt{i\,:\,'I\_m} to \texttt{'I\_n}
    keeping the same numerical value. Pure cast.

  \item[\texttt{lift (p\,:\,'I\_n.+1) j}] (\texttt{fintype.v})
    Casts \texttt{j\,:\,'I\_n} to \texttt{'I\_n.+1} ``skipping''
    the value $p$. With $p = $ \texttt{ord0}, this is $j \mapsto j+1$.

  \item[\texttt{unlift p i\,:\,option 'I\_n}] (\texttt{fintype.v})
    Partial inverse of \texttt{lift p}. Returns \texttt{None} when
    $i = p$, else \texttt{Some j} with $i = $ \texttt{lift p j}.

  \item[\texttt{rev\_ord i}] (\texttt{fintype.v}) The involution
    $i \mapsto n - 1 - i$ on \texttt{'I\_n}.

  \item[\texttt{\{set T\}}] (\texttt{finset.v}) The type of finite
    subsets of \texttt{T} (a finite type), with extensional equality.

  \item[\texttt{[set i\,:\,T\,|\,P i]}] (\texttt{finset.v}) Set
    comprehension: \texttt{\{i\,:\,T\,|\,P i\}}.

  \item[\texttt{[set f i\,|\,i in S]}] (\texttt{finset.v}) Image of
    set $S$ under function $f$.

  \item[\texttt{\#|S|}] (\texttt{fintype.v}) Cardinality of a finite
    set or predicate.

  \item[\texttt{i \textbackslash in S}] (\texttt{finset.v})
    Membership test, returning \texttt{bool}.

  \item[\texttt{\textasciitilde:\,S}] (\texttt{finset.v}) Complement
    of $S$ in its ambient finite type.

  \item[\texttt{\{perm T\}}] (\texttt{perm.v}) Type of bijections
    \texttt{T\,\textbackslash to T} for \texttt{T} a finite type.
    Inhabitant: a function plus its proof of injectivity.

  \item[\texttt{1\%g}] (\texttt{fingroup.v}) Group identity. For
    \texttt{\{perm T\}}, the identity permutation.

  \item[\texttt{(s * t)\%g}] (\texttt{fingroup.v}) Group
    multiplication. For perms: \texttt{(s * t) i = t (s i)}; see
    lemma \texttt{permM}.

  \item[\texttt{perm (h\,:\,injective f)}] (\texttt{perm.v})
    Constructor: turn an injective function into a \texttt{\{perm T\}}.

  \item[\texttt{\textbackslash sum\_(i\,:\,T) f i}] (\texttt{bigop.v})
    Sum of \texttt{f i} over every \texttt{i\,:\,T}.

  \item[\texttt{\textbackslash sum\_(i\,<\,n) f i}] (\texttt{bigop.v})
    Same, indexed by \texttt{i\,:\,'I\_n}.

  \item[\texttt{\textbackslash sum\_(i\,\textbackslash in\,S) f i}]
    (\texttt{bigop.v}) Sum over the elements of an explicit set
    \texttt{S}.

  \item[\texttt{\textbackslash sum\_(i\,|\,P i) f i}]
    (\texttt{bigop.v}) Sum over $i$ satisfying boolean filter $P$.
    The vertical bar is a filter, not disjunction.

  \item[\texttt{partition\_big f xpredT}] (\texttt{bigop.v})
    Rewrite a sum as a nested sum partitioned by the value of $f$.

  \item[\texttt{n.+1}, \texttt{n.+2}, \texttt{n.-1}] (\texttt{ssrnat.v})
    Successor, double-successor, predecessor on \texttt{nat}. Read
    as $n+1$, $n+2$, $n-1$.

  \item[\texttt{\textasciitilde\textasciitilde\,b}] Boolean negation.

  \item[\texttt{a\,==\,b}] (\texttt{eqtype.v}) Decidable equality
    on an \texttt{eqType}, returning \texttt{bool}. Distinct from
    propositional \texttt{=}; bridged by lemma \texttt{eqP}.

  \item[\texttt{Section X.\,Variable n.\,\ldots\,End X.}] (Rocq)
    Sections introduce parameters that are auto-generalised on
    \texttt{End}. Inside the section, \texttt{n} reads as a fixed
    natural; outside, every definition is implicitly parameterised.
\end{description}
